A call from a debt collector can be stressful, especially if you do not recognize the debt. The good news is that the **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)** and the rules that go with it give you real protections.

These rules mainly apply to third-party collectors and debt buyers, not usually to the original lender collecting its own debt. Many states add extra protections of their own.

## You have a right to details about the debt

Shortly after first contacting you, a collector has to give you a **validation notice**. It should show:

- How much the collector says you owe, and how that amount was worked out
- The name of the creditor
- How to dispute the debt, and the deadline for doing so

## You can dispute it

If you think the debt is not yours, or the amount is wrong, dispute it **in writing** within the time limit on the notice (normally 30 days after you receive it). The collector then has to stop collecting until it sends you verification. Keep copies of everything and use a delivery method that gives you proof of sending.

## Limits on contact

Collectors generally cannot:

- Call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your local time, unless you agree
- Contact you at work if they know your employer does not allow it
- Call over and over to harass you. Federal rules presume that more than seven calls in seven days about one debt is too many
- Discuss your debt with your family, friends or neighbors (they may contact others only to find your address or phone number)

You can also tell a collector **in writing** to stop contacting you. After that, it may only contact you to confirm it is stopping or to tell you about a specific action, such as a lawsuit.

## Things they can never do

Collectors cannot threaten violence or arrest, use obscene language, pretend to be lawyers or government officials, or threaten action they cannot legally take.

## Before you pay anything

- **Check the statute of limitations.** Old debts may be too old to sue over, and in some states a small payment can restart the clock.
- **Never ignore a lawsuit.** If you are sued, respond by the deadline, or the collector can win automatically.

If a collector breaks the rules, you can file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and your state attorney general.
